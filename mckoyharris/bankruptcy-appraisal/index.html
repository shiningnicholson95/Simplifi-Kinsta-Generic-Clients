What Is a Bankruptcy Appraisal?

The As-Is Value Evidence Required by Federal Bankruptcy Court

A bankruptcy appraisal is a certified, USPAP-compliant opinion of a property's as-is fair market value as of the date of filing or a court-specified effective date. Federal bankruptcy courts in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania require that real property valuations be established by a qualified appraiser — not by tax assessments, automated estimates, or informal broker opinions. The as-is standard means the value reflects the property in its actual current condition, regardless of needed repairs or deferred maintenance.

Reports are delivered with the documentation required by federal court — USPAP compliance certification, appraiser license, and E&O insurance documentation.

When You Need This

Six Bankruptcy Situations That Require a Certified Appraisal

Chapter 7 Liquidation (Homestead Exemption)Establishing the as-is market value to determine whether the available exemptions protect your equity — or whether surplus equity exists for the trustee to administer.

Chapter 13 Repayment PlanProviding the certified as-is value used to calculate the amount creditors are entitled to receive under the best-interests-of-creditors test — ensuring your plan is confirmable and defensible at the confirmation hearing.

Lien Stripping (Chapter 13)Establishing that the current as-is value of the property is at or below the balance owed on the first mortgage — making a junior lien (second mortgage, HELOC) wholly unsecured and eligible for stripping through the Chapter 13 plan.

Cramdown of Investment PropertyEstablishing as-is market value to support a cramdown motion for investment or rental property, reducing the secured portion of a lien to actual current market value when the loan balance exceeds property value.

Automatic Stay Lift MotionWhen a secured creditor moves to lift the automatic stay to proceed with foreclosure, a certified as-is appraisal is used to establish the value of the collateral — either supporting or opposing the motion depending on equity position.

Bankruptcy Schedules (Schedule A/B)Accurate as-is property values required on bankruptcy schedules filed with the court — establishing the asset inventory for estate administration and creditor review from the outset of the case.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common Questions About Bankruptcy Appraisals

Federal bankruptcy law requires an accurate, as-is opinion of property value when real estate is included in a bankruptcy estate. The value determines how much equity exists, whether a homestead exemption applies, whether a junior lien can be stripped, and what plan payments are required in Chapter 13. Courts and trustees do not accept Zillow estimates or tax assessments — they require a certified, USPAP-compliant appraisal from a licensed appraiser.

Pennsylvania does not have a state homestead exemption. Most debtors filing in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania (which covers Chester, Delaware, Montgomery, and Philadelphia counties) elect the federal bankruptcy exemptions instead, which protect only a limited, periodically adjusted amount of equity in a primary residence. Married couples who own their home as tenants by the entireties may have additional protection under Pennsylvania law. Because the protection is limited, a certified as-is appraisal establishing current market value is essential to determine how much equity exists and whether any of it is exposed to the trustee. Your bankruptcy attorney will confirm which exemptions apply to your case.

Lien stripping is a Chapter 13 tool that eliminates a wholly unsecured junior lien — such as a second mortgage or HELOC — when the home's current value is at or below the first mortgage balance. A certified appraisal establishing the as-is market value is the evidence used to demonstrate that the junior lien is fully unsecured and eligible for stripping. The appraisal must be current and USPAP-compliant to withstand a creditor challenge.

Cramdown allows a debtor to reduce the secured portion of a lien to the property's current fair market value. For primary residences, cramdown is generally prohibited under the anti-modification rule — but it applies to investment and rental properties and certain multi-unit scenarios. A certified as-is appraisal is the primary evidence establishing the cramdown value proposed in the Chapter 13 plan, and it must withstand creditor scrutiny.
